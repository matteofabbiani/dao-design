From a kitchen of nine to a table of sixteen

The story

Nir Mesika grew up in a house where his grandmother raised nine children and the older ones cooked for the younger. His grandfather, a pastry chef from Morocco, had opened a bakery there. His mother passed the recipes on. Dao is that kitchen, moved to an island.

The route

Tel AvivCulinary school, then the first kitchens in the city he grew up next to. The Moroccan flavours from home never left the plate.01
MilanA city that taught restraint. Fewer things on the plate, each one done properly.02
New YorkZizi Limona in Williamsburg, then Timna in the East Village. Reviewed twice by the New York Times, listed in the Michelin Guide.03
Koh PhanganHe chose the island over the city. One table, sixteen seats, and he comes out to it every evening.04

The morning decides the evening

Fish comes off the boats at Chaloklum, greens from the gardens around Sri Thanu, fruit from the market in Thong Sala. Nir does the round himself and is back in the kitchen by noon: drying, fermenting, pickling, the slow work that gives the evening its depth. What is found decides what is cooked, so the menu moves with the week.

The cooking is Thai and Levantine. The Israeli and Moroccan dishes he grew up with, made from Thai produce with Thai suppliers. Nothing is flown in that can be found on the island.

In print

Timna was reviewed by the New York Times and The New Yorker in the same month, and listed in the Michelin Guide. Nir left it for an island with one road.

Made on the island

Almost everything in the room was made on Koh Phangan or elsewhere in Thailand. The plates are thrown by a potter in Sri Thanu, each one slightly different. The one table was built on the island from a single tree, long enough for sixteen. The kitchen island was made by the same hands.

That is where the food comes from. The evening is where it goes.
